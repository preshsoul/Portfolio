I had seen the peach room long before I understood why I kept thinking about it.

Anthony Edwards is sitting alone in Adidas's _No Lie_ campaign, a lie detector somewhere beside him, answering questions which seem designed to give him opportunities to behave like a professional athlete being interviewed by a large company. Is this player better than you? What about that one? Are the AE 1s the best shoes in basketball? There is a familiar route available to him through every question. Praise the competition. Laugh. Say everybody is talented. Mention the work still to be done.

Edwards doesn't really take it.

What I remember most is not any particular boast. It is the mildness of his delivery. There is none of the strain that comes when somebody is trying to convince you of something outrageous. He smiles, pauses, answers. The machine beside him gradually becomes funny because it is being asked to certify something Edwards has already made obvious. You could remove it from the room and very little would change.

The room itself barely changes either. Peach behind him. Edwards. The shoe. Enough empty space for all three to become difficult to separate in your memory.

Then I watched it again, and again, and again. The superstar, imprinted in my mind.

Then I watched some of the earlier AE 1 work, where Adidas had already put Edwards against the same stripped-back visual world and given him rival signature shoes to dismiss. The campaign was confrontational, but what struck me was how little decoration the confrontation needed. Nothing resembling a heroic origin story had been built around him. There was no solemn voice telling us that greatness requires sacrifice. Edwards mostly needed somewhere to sit.

Months earlier, at USA Basketball's 50th-anniversary celebration in Las Vegas, Barack Obama had been talking about the talent on the Olympic team when Edwards interrupted him.

> "I'm the truth! These boys know!"

Obama laughed. So did everybody else.

The exchange happened before the Paris Olympics and before another layer of accomplishment could be added retrospectively to Edwards's confidence. It mattered to me for that reason. All the marketing campaigns implemented by the combination of his team and adidas began to take shape to me as the excavation of the character of a person, as opposed to the manufactured persona that you can smell from a mile away.

Advertisers have exhausted the word _authentic_ by now. Give somebody regional slang, imperfect lighting and permission to laugh at the camera and somebody in a meeting will eventually describe the result as authentic, unique, original, and every other adjective. Edwards's appeal is different from that because there is already so much performance in being Anthony Edwards. The outrageous prediction, the grin after it, the delight in being challenged, the next prediction. Confidence can be natural and still become a routine, and once an audience learns which version of you it likes best, you learn something too.

Adidas's work with him as I have realised, has gradually begun to find it's place somewhere inside that loop.

With a persona and a brand, every element, regardless of how "minor" it might seem, contributes significantly to what is being created, and thus, with the first AE 1, we all knew there was something coming. It was difficult to confuse with the signature shoes surrounding it, with the moulded side panels and grille-like shape, and at [$120](https://boardroom.tv/cover-story-anthony-edwards-adidas-new-blueprint/) it remained within sight of the young basketball player Adidas clearly wanted to reach. Colourways kept drawing on Georgia. Then, against Utah in March 2024, Edwards wore the Georgia Red Clay pair and climbed over John Collins for one of those dunks whose violence makes everybody in the photograph look temporarily arranged around the person in the air.

No campaign could have commissioned that moment, infact, Adidas could only be lucky enough to have built something ready to receive it.

The shoe was on his feet. Georgia was in the colour. The image went everywhere. The confidence in the advertisements no longer existed only in advertisements.

For a while I thought this was the whole thing, finally, Adidas had found an athlete with a personality unusually difficult to sand down, then resisted sanding it down.

The stranger thing is how little Adidas has required the advertisements around Edwards to resemble one another.

The peach room is the easiest image to remember, but it has never really been a prison. One of the earliest AE 1 commercials has Edwards opening a bag filled with other players' signature shoes. LeBron's first shoe comes out. Then Ja Morant's, Luka Dončić's, LaMelo Ball's. He looks at them with approximately the amount of consideration you would give somebody offering you the wrong flavour of crisps, dismisses them and eventually reaches his own. There is barely a premise beyond the sort of argument people have about shoes anyway.

Later the premise became considerably more elaborate.

In _Man Down_, Ice-T turns up as a detective investigating what appears to be a crime scene. Edwards has been leaving bodies behind after dunks. Yellow tape appears. Evidence is collected. The AE 1 becomes part weapon, part exhibit. Adidas eventually carried the fiction into the Target Center during the playoffs, where shoes were displayed like forensic evidence, fans could recreate the offending dunk and purchases left inside evidence bags.

By then it was difficult to know where the advertisement stopped.

The next year Edwards was standing in front of an old arcade machine.

Someone asks whom he is trying to beat when he already has the high score.

"Myself, crazy man."

The game supplies everybody else anyway.

The first opponent is impossibly long, green and alien-looking. His potential is almost limitless. His swag is zero. His shoe size is twenty-seven. Edwards tells him to find the weight room. Another character possesses unlimited strength but takes an eternity at the free-throw line and seems permanently surrounded by trade rumours. The next has accumulated too many MVPs and an unusual enthusiasm for horses. Another stands suspiciously close to a referee, with a whistle listed among his accessories. Luka arrives near the end already exhausted.

Nobody has to be named, that is part of the joke.

The advertisement assumes you have been following the same stupid, repetitive and occasionally very funny basketball conversations everybody else has. Wembanyama is the alien. Jokić would rather be with his horses. Shai gets a whistle. Luka is tired, and the best part? Adidas does not explain the references because explaining them would ruin the feeling that the campaign has simply wandered into a conversation already in progress.

It is also a fairly dangerous way for a multinational company to advertise a shoe. Signature-athlete campaigns normally have good reasons to avoid insulting the other signature athletes, future teammates, league MVPs and enormously valuable people with their own sponsorship arrangements, but in a stroke of unintended genius, Adidas appears to have decided that excessive politeness would be more damaging to Edwards's character than the occasional offended fan.

The formats keep becoming stranger.

For the cheaper Believe That. 1, Ant (Edwards) appeared inside something resembling an old workout tape or late-night television infomercial. Billy Blanks turns up. Other Adidas athletes drift through it. A phone number appears on screen: `1-855-BLEEDAT`. You could actually call it and reach Edwards's recorded phone tree.

During an August trip to Manila, the joke became cheaper in the best possible sense. Instead of licensing the league's biggest players, Adidas found lookalikes. Edwards played against approximate versions of Luka Dončić, Stephen Curry and LeBron James wearing uniforms close enough for everybody to understand what was happening.

The campaign has therefore done something I initially missed when I kept returning to the peach room. Adidas did build Edwards a recognisable visual language, but it has been remarkably willing to abandon the visual language whenever there is a better joke.

What remains is harder to photograph.

He is usually arguing with somebody, even when nobody else is in the room. There is always another player who supposedly cannot guard him, another shoe he does not rate, another reason his own confidence should become ridiculous if he did not deliver it so casually. The advertisements can become a police procedural, an arcade game or a bad television broadcast and still feel as though the same person has somehow taken control of them.

That made the campaign more interesting to me than consistency normally is.

The consistency was Edwards. The person.
## The passenger seat

In 2025, Adidas put Ant in a car with Nick Maddox, one of his closest friends, his childhood best friend as they sang Fantasia's "When I See U." Nothing exactly spectacular happens, but it was emotive, and at the core, it is part of why the scene stays with me.

The peach room had isolated Edwards until almost everything in the image belonged to him, the interaction in the car does the opposite. He has to share the space not with just another person however, this is someone who has known him all his life. Maddox knows him well enough not to behave as though proximity to an NBA star is remarkable, which makes our own proximity feel less remarkable too, this was jsut a normal guy, playing ball with his best friend, just like most people did. Adidas later stretched that idea into [_Anthony Edwards: Illuminated_](https://news.adidas.com/global/anthony-edwards--by-his-best-friend-nick-maddox/s/9b5c6942-87fc-4643-8a4b-e96ff5f45b2e), a film told substantially through Maddox rather than Edwards himself.

There are stories about car rides, school, basketball, family and grief. Maddox has memories from before a commercial relationship had any reason to care about them, and at various points the film can feel almost too convenient in this regard. A sportswear company has been allowed into a friendship and has returned with exactly the sort of emotional material that makes an athlete easier to love.

Grief does not become less private because the person grieving later signs an endorsement contract. Friendship does not exist to provide character references for famous people, and as I like to say, there is always something peculiar about a company willingly spending millions of dollars to make access feel unbought, natural.

But _Illuminated_ works better than it reasonably should because Maddox is allowed to remain more than evidence for Edwards's goodness. He is not a celebrity interviewer performing intimacy for us. He interrupts, remembers, laughs and carries parts of the story himself. Adidas has produced the situation, chosen the footage and shaped the final film, but Edwards is no longer the only person with narrative authority.

That changed what I thought the earlier campaign was doing.

Perhaps the appeal was not Edwards's personality by itself. Personality had attracted me to the room, but the films kept finding ways of putting that personality in relation to something outside itself: Atlanta, the shoe, an argument about another player, somebody who knew him before all of this.

One Adidas executive described the strange balance around Edwards by saying that kids feel as though they can touch him. Edwards possesses a body and athletic ability almost nobody watching him will ever approach. Adidas has no interest in making that part ordinary. The distance remains. What keeps changing is everything around it.

Basketball is particularly good at producing this kind of contradiction.

Five people play at once, yet so much of the sport's public life bends toward one person. Faces are uncovered. Possessions become duels, isos, they are called. Almost every highlight reel often remove the other eight players entirely. A signature shoe puts one person's name under thousands of other people's feet. Fans can follow a player across teams and cities while continuing to claim that attachment as a form of loyalty rather than disloyalty.

Years ago Colin Cowherd made a complaint about Jayson Tatum that I initially found unfair.

> Think about Magic Johnson, you can see the smile.
> Think about Michael Jordan, you can see that cocky grin.
> Think about LeBron, you can see the imagery, the pregame chalk.
> You can close your eyes and there is images of stars.
> Close your eyes and do the same for Tatum, I don't see anything.
> -- Colin Cowherd on Jayson Tatum

Tatum was being compared with players whose public images had decades to percolate, gradual improvements, one performance after the other. Magic Johnson comes with that all too familiar smile. Jordan can arrive as a tongue, a silhouette, a body suspended in a move that defined Nike for generations. LeBron has accumulated enough images to furnish a museum.

Cowherd's comparison was premature at that point, still the mechanism interested me anyway. Sporting achievement often serve to empower the translation into memory. Statistics help. Championships help. Eventually something else happens. A movement, face, shoe, celebration or place begins arriving before the numbers do.

The Edwards campaign seems unusually conscious of that process. Every element that makes it way unto the screen can be remembered because they all served a purpose, reinforced an ideal, Peach becomes useful because it can be remembered, Maddox becomes useful for fiving the perception in our mind, an ordinary, real person perecption that every individual can cling to.

And then Adidas took almost the same question into football, where the individual is harder to detach from everything around him.

## The backyard

Football has never lacked superstars, it has produced some of the most recognisable people alive, Pele, Messi, Cristiano Ronaldo, and even as the sports continues to maintain its spot as the greatest sport on earth, it keeps making the star answer to things older than himself.

The shirt existed first. So did the club. So did the national team, the stadium, the neighbourhood and the songs. A great player can transform all of them and still leave after four seasons. Somebody else will wear the number. Somebody's father was standing on the same terrace before the player was born.

Basketball has histories like this too, and football obviously has fans devoted almost entirely to individual players. I am describing a difference of emphasis rather than two laws of nature. Still, watch how the sports tend to photograph greatness and the difference becomes difficult to ignore. Basketball advertising has spent decades making the athlete large enough to fill the frame. Football keeps discovering people behind him.

This became more obvious to me watching Adidas's broader _You Got This_ work.

The company introduced the platform in 2024 after research into the pressure surrounding sport, particularly the way young people were losing some of the pleasure that had brought them to it. Elite players were returned to parks, streets and improvised spaces. In 2025 the camera moved outward again. Parents, friends, coaches and teammates became part of the explanation for how pressure is created and how it can be relieved, Maddox belongs to that movement, he brought us all into that moment where Ant spoke about his family, the pressure, unspoken, the journey and impact of the grief of losing his mother and grandmother prior to even getting drafted, the sacrifice he gives so selflessly to his family, the relationship with his daughter. This is true showing of a regular guy, the superstar, but still the regular guy, just like that one guy you know in your neighbourhood who has the highest chances of reaching the peak.

By May 2026, just before the first match of the World Cup, adidas found another backyard.

## Another backyard

[_Backyard Legends_](https://news.adidas.com/world-cup-2026/adidas-presents-backyard-legends-with-icons-from-football--music--and-film-coming-together-to-celebr/s/0a1745f6-5a88-473b-93f7-8ce53a52d4dd) begins with Timothée Chalamet trying to assemble a football team. He has an absurd number of options. Lionel Messi. Lamine Yamal. Jude Bellingham. Trinity Rodman. Other players and famous people continue wandering into a film which seems determined to find out how many celebrities can be squeezed into five minutes before celebrity itself becomes background noise.

The opponents are Clive, Ruthie and Isaak.

They are local, they have their own pitch. They have apparently been beating visitors there for thirty years. Beckham, Del Piero and Zidane belong to the story as famous men who have already tried and failed.

I had expected the joke to be that ordinary people were suddenly sharing a pitch with extraordinary players but the film kept defying the expectation, rearranging things the other way round. Chalamet's team are the visitors. The locals own the record, the ground and the rumours.

For all its excess, the arrangement feels recognisable. Most people who have played football casually know some version of the unbeatable person attached to a particular patch of ground. His professional prospects are irrelevant to the story. Somebody once saw him score from somewhere impossible. Somebody else insists he nearly joined an academy. He may now be forty-three and limping slightly. None of this has reduced the legend.

Football is full of these local jurisdictions.

It is why the image of Ousmane Dembélé taking the 2025 Ballon d'Or back to Évreux made immediate sense to me. The trophy is designed to prop one player from everybody else in the world and call him the best. In a lot of sense, bringing it [the trophy] home does something peculiar to that isolation. Streets and people return to the frame, and that individual achievement acquires witnesses who can remember the person before the achievement existed.

None of this requires the superstar to become ordinary. Dembélé remains the man holding the Ballon d'Or. Messi remains Messi when he arrives at a backyard.

Somewhere while thinking about this, I remembered two boys picking football teams in an Adidas commercial from 2006.

In [_José +10_](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=af5sCvOVWOY), José and Pedro stand on a neighbourhood pitch and begin choosing players. The selections quickly become something out of their wildest dreams. Kaká arrives. Beckham arrives. Ballack, Zidane and others materialise into the sort of game children construct when the available evidence is limited only by imagination. The stars are enormous, but the fantasy still belongs to the boys. Famous superstars, all report for duty inside a children's imagination.

I had remembered the advertisement fondly without thinking very hard about its relationship with the current one. Noe, watching the two close together made the connection so much harder to dismiss. Twenty years ago, the children summoned the stars onto their pitch. In 2026, Chalamet spends the film trying to recruit stars capable of surviving somebody else's.

The relationship is different now,.

That does not make _Backyard Legends_ a remake, and there is enough nostalgia slathered across the new film that the comparison can become too easy. The clothes, old technology, music and street imagery point backwards aggressively. Some of its 1990s world belongs to people who remember the decade; some belongs to people who have inherited the decade as pictures, faint memories of childhood. The ordinariness of this kind of inherited nostalgia.

People miss football shirts released before they were born, buy cameras whose technical limitations have become aesthetic advantages, and listen to stories about public spaces they never occupied. Memory is no longer required to begin with personal experience, in the real world, sometimes an image arrives first before the memory is constructed around it.

That is why José +10 matters more to me than the old styling.

Adidas was not merely trying to recreate a visual moodboard, tapping into your nostalgia, a visual mood. It recovered an orientation of people: the local game, global star, child, imagination, place, then it changed who needed admission.

What looked new had an generational precedence.

## What a brand remembers

Adidas has often been at its most culturally important when people found meanings for its products that the company did not fully control. The old Run-DMC story survives because of the asymmetry. A sportswear company discovered that people had already turned its shoes into something else. Terrace culture did similar things to products intended for sport, same as the Samba has spent decades accumulating uses and associations that nobody at Adidas could have placed ideated seamlessly inside an original product brief.

There is a temptation to treat this history as proof that Adidas understands culture better than its competitors. but history, as you may know is not that cooperative.

Nike helped create much of the athlete mythology Adidas is now responding to. Jordan, the solitary runner, the athlete enlarged into a cultural protagonist: the modern sports advertisement owes Nike far too much for the company to be reduced to the boring villain in somebody else's comeback story.

Scale does introduce a problem, though, the more athletes and products pass through a system, the easier it becomes for a method that once depended on close observation to become a reusable format.

Stephen Curry discovered the humiliating version of this in 2013.

During Nike's pitch to keep him, an official reportedly mispronounced his first name and a presentation slide still contained Kevin Durant's name. The anecdote has acquired symbolic weight because it is almost too perfectly designed for the purpose. Curry was sitting in a room where a company was asking for his future while accidentally showing him the previous person in the template.

That meeting cannot explain Nike's subsequent history. It doesn't need to.

I think about it while looking at the peach room because the commercial environment Adidas built around Edwards would be strangely difficult to hand to somebody else. You could reproduce the set. You could copy the lighting. The rest would become imitation very quickly.

This creates its own danger, as soon as everybody learns that Edwards's confidence is marketable, confidence becomes something the market keeps demanding from Edwards. Nike supplied a useful demonstration during the 2026 playoffs. After Victor Wembanyama and San Antonio eliminated Minnesota, Nike answered Adidas's _Believe That_ language with _Believe This_. The phrase Adidas had worked to make recognisable around Edwards became convenient precisely because a rival could now turn it against him.

That is what happens when the marketing works.

A public character has become prominent enough to parody.

There is no untouched version of authenticity waiting outside this system. Somebody chose the questions in _No Lie_. Somebody placed the lie detector there. Somebody knew how long to hold the pause. _Illuminated_ contains friendship, but friendship has been filmed, selected and edited. _Backyard Legends_ may celebrate the ungoverned pleasure of a local game, but its backyard contains Messi, Chalamet and one of the largest sports companies in the world.

The best question I think anyone can ask is probably not whether any of this is real, it is what remains of that true persona, the essence of the person which survives the widening maw of specially curated industrial construction.

Edwards boasting in _No Lie_ has equivalents outside the commercial. The confidence existed in the room with Obama and in interviews Adidas did not direct. The Georgia references around the shoe can attach themselves to a sporting moment nobody could plan. Maddox's relationship with Edwards existed before Adidas had a campaign that needed it.

The backyard is more artificial, yet the social logic beneath it is familiar. Walls become touchlines. Teams are argued over. Somebody owns the ball and therefore possesses an amount of administrative power wildly disproportionate to his ability. The best player nearby acquires a mythology which may never appear on the internet.

This is also why community becomes the hardest part of the campaign to fake over time. A company can borrow the appearance of a public pitch for five minutes. It cannot permanently borrow the relationship people have with that pitch. At some point, the work outside the camera starts to matter: access, facilities, local adaptations, the things that remain after the production leaves.

Advertising has always been capable of making the expensive look intimate. The more interesting development here is that Adidas keeps allowing intimacy to come from somewhere it does not wholly own.

A friend.

A city.

A neighbourhood.

An archive.

I noticed something else only after spending too much time with these campaigns.

Nike's most famous line tells you to _Just Do It_., Adidas keeps saying _You Got This_.

Both taglines are just three words. One pushes. The other assumes.

I don't think slogans can bear the weight of an entire theory of two multinational companies, and it would be silly to pretend thirty-seven years of Nike advertising can be understood from the grammar of one sentence. Still, the difference felt connected to what I had been watching. So much of classical athlete marketing asks the product to shorten the distance between the person you are and the person on the screen. The shoe belongs to the person you might become.

The Adidas work I kept returning to was something different entirely, Edwards already sounds like somebody you know. Maddox occupies the passenger seat. The football pitch belongs to locals before the professionals arrive. José and Pedro choose the teams, and it all reminds us, that the archive is full of products whose cultural lives exceeded their intended ones.

The audience keeps being given something it can claim before it is asked to admire.

Whether that can actually be converted into a repeatable corporate strategy is another question. Attempts to manufacture recognition tend to become extremely embarrassing once the audience notices the manufacture. There are already plenty of advertisements full of grain, accents, neighbourhood courts and conspicuously imperfect bedrooms created by people who would panic if anything on set were genuinely uncontrolled.

Adidas still has to sell things.

The commercial evidence around the current period is encouraging, although it refuses to cooperate with any simple claim about advertising effectiveness. The Adidas brand grew 13 per cent on a currency-neutral basis in 2024, with the AE 1 named among products contributing to performance-basketball growth. Performance revenue increased another 15 per cent in 2025. During the World Cup quarter in 2026, company revenue reached a record €6.7 billion, direct-to-consumer sales rose 25 per cent and the performance business grew 39 per cent, led by football and running.

It would be absurd to attribute those figures to _Backyard Legends_, _No Lie_ or any other film. Adidas had products people wanted, tournament exposure, distribution, fourteen federation kits at the World Cup, sponsorship inventory, retail operations and competitors making their own mistakes. It also spent another €212 million on World Cup marketing in that quarter. Attention remains expensive even when the advertisements look effortless.

The AE 1 is useful again because the pieces can be seen meeting in one place. The design was recognisable before the campaign asked anybody to invest meaning in it. Edwards could produce live sporting moments while wearing it. The price allowed younger players to imagine owning it. The advertising gave those pieces a common visual memory.

The story did not have to carry the product by itself.

Neither does the neighbourhood.

That is perhaps where I had been going wrong when I first watched Edwards sitting against peach and thought Adidas had simply discovered a more convincing form of athlete authenticity. The campaigns kept widening the picture until authenticity became too small a word for what was happening.

Edwards had a voice. Then somebody who remembered that voice from earlier entered the frame.

Football had superstars. Then the ground beneath them became part of the story.

The new campaign had a backyard. Then a twenty-year-old advertisement appeared behind it.

Eventually the thing I kept noticing was less about how closely Adidas could bring us to famous people than about how often it gave greatness somewhere to come from.

I think again about Cowherd asking people to close their eyes and picture a superstar.

The obvious images are usually the finished ones: Jordan in the air, the viral Messi picture where he was laying in bed next to his world cup, the 'Sui' celebration by Cristiano copied by children who have never met he who invented it. Advertising wants those pictures badly. They travel. They sell. They compress years into seconds.

But there is another image Adidas keeps returning to.

Before the arena, somebody is in a car.

Before the World Cup, somebody has a patch of ground whose dimensions have been agreed upon through argument.

Before a shoe becomes an icon, somebody wears it for a reason the company did not invent.

And before the superstar becomes large enough to fill the frame, there are people and places around him which remember when he didn't.
